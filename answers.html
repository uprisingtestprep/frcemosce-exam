  1. Station 1: Anticoagulated melaena with an uncertain disposition (Circuit 1, Slot 1)

    Station Category: Gastrointestinal Bleeding, Anticoagulation, Risk Stratification

    Domains Assessed: Communication including conflict, History taking and information gathering, Clinical reasoning and decision making, Organisation and prioritisation

    Partner or Colleague Role Script

    You are 74-year-old Margaret, anxious and embarrassed by the black stool. Opening: 'I nearly fainted in the bathroom. Does the blood thinner mean I am bleeding inside?' If asked about amount, say there was a large black stool and you feel light-headed when standing. You have not vomited blood. You took your usual apixaban yesterday. Disclose previous peptic ulcer only if asked about stomach problems or prior bleeding. If asked about chest pain or breathlessness, say neither is present. You become tearful when discussing going home alone and want reassurance about who will look after you.

    Examiner Checklist

    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Recognises suspected upper gastrointestinal bleeding and treats the NEWS2 score as an underestimate because of the collapse and postural symptoms.
    • History taking and information gathering: Asks about haematemesis, stool volume and frequency, syncope, prior ulcer or bleeding, alcohol, liver disease and adherence to apixaban.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Checks airway and breathing, pulse character, capillary refill, abdominal tenderness and signs of chronic liver disease before proposing investigation.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Requests large-bore intravenous access, repeat observations, full blood count, coagulation screen, renal and liver tests, group and save and a venous blood gas.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Does not transfuse solely because the haemoglobin is 89 g/L, and states that transfusion should follow symptoms, ongoing loss and a senior decision.
    • Communication including conflict: Explains that apixaban may need temporary review but will not be stopped permanently without the bleeding and specialist teams agreeing.
    • Organisation and prioritisation: Requests urgent gastroenterology and critical care review and admits for monitored care and early endoscopy according to local major bleeding guidance.
    • Communication including conflict: Gives a clear closing plan covering observation, further tests, possible endoscopy, medication review and escalation if bleeding or dizziness worsens.

    Model Answer

    I would introduce myself, check that Margaret is comfortable and take a focused history while the nurse repeats observations and places two large-bore cannulas. I need to establish whether she has haematemesis, ongoing melaena, abdominal pain, previous ulcers, liver disease, alcohol use or another bleeding source. Her apixaban, renal function and last dose matter, as does any antiplatelet use. The brief collapse and postural symptoms mean this is clinically significant bleeding even though the NEWS2 score is only 1. I would examine for shock, abdominal tenderness and chronic liver disease, then request full blood count, coagulation, renal and liver tests, group and save, and a venous blood gas. She needs a monitored bed and urgent senior emergency medicine review. I would discuss early gastroenterology input for endoscopy and critical care support according to local major bleeding guidance. I would not reverse apixaban automatically for a stable non-catastrophic bleed or transfuse for the haemoglobin value alone. The bleeding team and haematology should guide temporary withholding or reversal, with attention to thrombosis risk. I would explain that the likely source is above the bowel and that endoscopy may find and treat it. The safe disposition is admission for repeated observations, blood tests and specialist review. I would tell Margaret who will review her, what symptoms require immediate escalation and that the team will speak with her family about support at home.

    References Provided

    Local major gastrointestinal bleeding pathway and the patient's anticoagulation clinic letter.

  2. Station 2: Confusion in decompensated liver disease (Circuit 1, Slot 2)

    Station Category: Liver Disease, Alcohol Withdrawal, Delirium

    Domains Assessed: History taking and information gathering, Clinical examination, Clinical reasoning and decision making, Organisation and prioritisation

    Partner or Colleague Role Script

    You are Daniel, aged 52, brought by a neighbour because you are confused. Opening: 'I do not need a fuss. I just cannot get my words out.' Answer questions slowly. Hidden information: last alcohol was three days ago; you have tremor, sweats and saw insects on the wall last night; your neighbour found you on the floor but you did not lose consciousness. Disclose the fall only if asked about injury or events today. Admit poor sleep and poor eating when asked. If challenged kindly about alcohol, become defensive, then acknowledge drinking a bottle of spirits most days until three days ago.

    Examiner Checklist

    • History taking and information gathering: Obtains collateral history about the time course of confusion, alcohol intake, last drink, falls, head injury, medication adherence and bowel frequency.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Treats hypoglycaemia, sepsis, hypoxia, drug toxicity, stroke and intracranial injury as reversible causes to exclude before labelling the confusion as hepatic encephalopathy.
    • Clinical examination: Performs a focused neurological examination including GCS, pupils, focal signs, tremor, asterixis and evidence of autonomic withdrawal.
    • Clinical examination: Examines for ascites, jaundice, bruising, injection marks and features of chronic liver disease, and checks for occult injury after the fall.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Requests full blood count, renal and liver profiles, clotting, ammonia if locally used, blood gas, lactate, glucose, blood cultures, urine testing and chest imaging where indicated.
    • Organisation and prioritisation: Starts a locally approved pathway for alcohol withdrawal with symptom monitoring and benzodiazepine treatment rather than using a fixed sedative regimen.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: States that parenteral thiamine should be given according to the local alcohol-related brain injury pathway and not delayed while waiting for a specialist review.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Discusses lactulose dosing, constipation, dehydration and electrolyte review, and avoids both over-diuresis and unsupported fluid overload.
    • Organisation and prioritisation: Admits to a monitored ward bed, requests early hepatology and mental health or alcohol liaison input, and gives clear instructions for review and escalation.

    Model Answer

    I would assess Daniel using an ABCDE approach while obtaining collateral history from his neighbour. Causes that must be excluded include hypoglycaemia, hypoxia, sepsis, stroke, head injury after the fall, medication toxicity, gastrointestinal bleeding and alcohol withdrawal. I would check his GCS and pupils, look for focal neurology, tremor and autonomic signs, and examine for asterixis, ascites, jaundice, bruising and signs of chronic liver disease. Hypoglycaemia is already excluded by the bedside glucose of 7.8 mmol/L, but I would still review the timing and reliability of measurements. I would request full blood count, renal and liver tests, clotting, blood gas, lactate, blood cultures and imaging determined by the examination. If ammonia is used locally, I would interpret it alongside the clinical picture rather than in isolation. Daniel has features of alcohol withdrawal after three days without alcohol, and possible hepatic encephalopathy. Both need active treatment. I would use the local symptom-triggered alcohol withdrawal pathway, give parenteral thiamine according to local policy and discuss lactulose after considering constipation and electrolytes. I would avoid admission under a vague label of confusion. He needs a monitored bed, repeated neurological observations, early hepatology input and alcohol liaison involvement. I would explain the provisional causes to Daniel and his neighbour, state that delirium can fluctuate, and arrange urgent senior review if his GCS falls, he develops fever, bleeding or focal neurological signs.

    References Provided

    Local alcohol withdrawal and decompensated liver disease pathways.

  3. Station 3: Late presentation after a paracetamol overdose (Circuit 1, Slot 3)

    Station Category: Toxicology, Paracetamol Overdose, Self-Harm Risk

    Domains Assessed: Practical and pinnacle skills, Clinical reasoning and decision making, Organisation and prioritisation, Communication including conflict

    Partner or Colleague Role Script

    Talk through the actions you would take now, using the chart to decide whether treatment is needed and identifying what information is still required. Explain the observations and blood results and show how you would arrange urgent transfer or admission.

    Examiner Checklist

    • Practical and pinnacle skills: Obtains the time of ingestion, stated dose, weight, co-ingestants, vomiting, liver disease and medication history before using the poisons chart.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Recognises that treatment must not wait when the interval, dose or history is uncertain and acetylcysteine is indicated by the local pathway.
    • Practical and pinnacle skills: Checks the local treatment chart and any updated poisons advice rather than relying on a remembered nomogram line.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Requests repeat full blood count, renal and liver tests, clotting, electrolytes and glucose, and explains that ALT and clotting need serial review.
    • Organisation and prioritisation: Arranges immediate senior and poisons advice for a late presentation with an abnormal ALT and starts treatment without avoidable delay.
    • Communication including conflict: Asks about intent, previous attempts, safeguarding concerns, access to medicines and a safe place to stay without sounding judgemental.
    • Organisation and prioritisation: Requests urgent mental health or crisis assessment and does not treat medical clearance as a substitute for an assessment of suicide risk.
    • Organisation and prioritisation: Explains the admission plan, monitoring, treatment duration and escalation triggers, and arranges appropriate transfer if the local unit cannot provide care.

    Model Answer

    I would first confirm the time, possible dose, patient weight and whether she vomited or took anything else. The precise ingestion is uncertain, the presentation is late and the ALT is already mildly abnormal, so I would not delay treatment while trying to make the numbers fit a remembered chart. I would use the current local poisons chart and contact the poisons service, then start acetylcysteine according to the approved local regimen. The chart determines whether further measurement or a modified regimen is needed. I would request repeat liver tests, clotting, renal function, electrolytes and glucose, and monitor for hepatic injury or a coagulopathy. I would examine for any other injuries and establish baseline observations. The overdose is only one part of the assessment. I would ask about intent, a suicide plan, previous attempts, access to medicines, substances and social support. I would involve the crisis team urgently and discuss safeguarding with a senior clinician. The safe disposition is admission to a unit able to continue acetylcysteine and serial organ function monitoring, with escalation to hepatology or a liver unit if the liver injury progresses. I would explain each step back to the patient, check her understanding, and arrange close observation while she is in the emergency department. I would also check the local pathway for transfer and communicate the plan clearly to receiving staff.

    References Provided

    Current local paracetamol overdose chart and poisons information service.

  4. Station 4: Demand to leave while hypoxic (Circuit 1, Slot 4)

    Station Category: Respiratory Infection, Communication, Conflict, Mental Capacity

    Domains Assessed: Communication including conflict, Clinical examination, Clinical reasoning and decision making, Organisation and prioritisation

    Partner or Colleague Role Script

    You are Paul, aged 46. You are frightened, breathing heavily and standing with your coat on. Opening: 'I am not staying. Give me tablets and oxygen for home.' You have a previous asthma admission and have been using a reliever inhaler more often. Hidden information: you are the sole carer for your father, you have no allocated work leave and you fear hospitals. Disclose these reasons only when asked what is worrying you or what makes leaving feel necessary. If the candidate explains risks calmly and offers a plan, soften and sit down. If they issue commands, raise your voice and move towards the curtain.

    Examiner Checklist

    • Communication including conflict: Uses a calm, non-judgemental opening, lowers the conflict, sits at the patient's level and asks what is driving the wish to leave.
    • Clinical examination: Reassesses airway, breathing, speech, respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, chest sounds and mental state before discussing discharge.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Identifies that the patient is hypoxic and tachycardic and activates the emergency team response, requests senior review, continuous monitoring, oxygen to the appropriate target and urgent investigation.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Considers acute severe asthma and pneumonia and arranges chest radiograph, blood tests and treatment according to the relevant local pathway.
    • Communication including conflict: Explains clearly that taking oxygen home is not a safe treatment and that leaving now could allow respiratory failure to progress.
    • Communication including conflict: Assesses capacity by checking whether Paul can understand, retain, weigh and communicate the decision, rather than equating an unwise choice with incapacity.
    • Organisation and prioritisation: Responds to the carer duty by contacting the nurse in charge, social work or the local carer support route while urgent treatment continues.
    • Communication including conflict: If Paul has capacity and refuses, respects the refusal and does not treat the deteriorating clinical risk as authority to override it; documents the discussion, seeks urgent senior review, addresses his carer responsibilities and offers treatment, review and immediate-return advice.
    • Organisation and prioritisation: Closes with a direct safety plan naming who will monitor him, what will happen next and which symptoms require an emergency call.

    Model Answer

    I would keep my voice calm and introduce myself while the nurse brings oxygen and continuous monitoring. I would not block the exit or make promises about admission before I know what Paul fears. I would ask why leaving feels necessary and acknowledge his responsibility for his father and his fear of hospitals. At the same time I would assess him: he is hypoxic, tachycardic and working hard to breathe. I would listen to the chest, check speech and mental state, activate the emergency team response and request senior help, start oxygen to the appropriate target and arrange focused tests and treatment for acute severe asthma or pneumonia. I would explain that oxygen cannot safely be supplied for home use through the emergency department and that leaving with a saturation of 90% could lead to worsening respiratory failure. I would assess capacity under the Mental Capacity Act by testing understanding, retention, use of information and communication. An unwise decision alone does not prove incapacity. If Paul has capacity and refuses admission, I still need urgent senior review and a documented risk discussion. I would offer treatment while he stays, contact the team responsible for his father and arrange the safest possible follow-up. If Paul remains capacitous, I would respect his refusal of admission and treatment and would not use the Mental Capacity Act, security or restraint to override it. I would seek urgent senior review, offer treatment while he is willing to stay, address his responsibility for his father and give immediate-return advice. If his capacity changes, I would reassess the specific decision. If he then lacks capacity and immediate treatment is necessary to save life or prevent serious harm, I would use the section 5 Mental Capacity Act route and the least restrictive action, with a lawful basis for any deprivation of liberty. Mental Health Act powers would be considered only if the statutory mental disorder criteria are met. My closing plan would name the monitoring, treatment, review and exact symptoms that require urgent help.

    References Provided

    UK acute asthma and oxygen therapy information plus the trust Mental Capacity Act policy.

  5. Station 5: Wrong patient label on a pre-transfusion sample (Circuit 1, Slot 5)

    Station Category: Transfusion Safety, Medical Error, Duty Of Candour

    Domains Assessed: Communication including conflict, Clinical reasoning and decision making, Organisation and prioritisation, Leadership and management

    Partner or Colleague Role Script

    You are Staff Nurse Aisha, tired and upset after a busy shift. Opening: 'The lab says the label is wrong. I took that sample. What happens now?' Hidden information: you labelled the tube in the treatment room while answering an emergency buzzer and did not check the wristband at the bedside. You are worried about being blamed. You want to know whether the patient is safe. If the candidate listens and asks for facts, explain your actions honestly. If confronted aggressively, become defensive and say nothing more.

    Examiner Checklist

    • Leadership and management: Makes immediate patient safety the first priority by confirming that no blood component has been released and pausing the transfusion plan.
    • Communication including conflict: Listens to Aisha without blaming her, acknowledges the pressure she is under and asks for a calm factual sequence of events.
    • Organisation and prioritisation: Requests a correctly labelled repeat group and save using full bedside identity checks, including asking the patient to state identifying details when possible.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Reviews whether the patient's anaemia is symptomatic enough to need urgent senior review while the repeat sample is processed.
    • Communication including conflict: Plans an honest duty-of-candour conversation with the patient, explaining what happened, that no blood was given and what safety checks are now in place.
    • Leadership and management: Escalates to the nurse in charge and transfusion laboratory or site coordinator and starts or supports the local incident report promptly.
    • Leadership and management: Identifies system factors such as interruption during labelling and separates learning from individual blame while still addressing unsafe practice.
    • Organisation and prioritisation: Closes by telling Aisha what will happen next, offering senior support, and naming the review, documentation and follow-up actions.

    Model Answer

    I would start by confirming that no unit has been collected or transfused and ask the laboratory to quarantine any associated request. Patient safety comes first. I would then ask Aisha to tell me what happened from start to finish. I would acknowledge that the department is busy and that she reported the problem promptly. The aim is not to blame her before the facts are known. I would send a repeat sample with a full bedside identity check, using the wristband and a second check rather than relying on a room label. I would contact the blood bank, tell the nurse in charge and review whether the patient needs urgent critical care or other treatment while we wait. I would speak to the patient with a senior colleague available. I would explain honestly that a sample was mislabelled, that no blood was transfused, that the error was found before harm and that we are repeating the tests safely. I would document that conversation. I would submit an incident report and ask the transfusion laboratory to review whether patient identification matched at every stage. The learning should address interruptions, labelling at the bedside and a clear stop-the-line rule. Aisha also needs support and a chance to contribute to the review. I would close by confirming the repeat sample result, the revised transfusion decision and who will follow up the incident.

    References Provided

    Trust blood transfusion policy, bedside identity-check standard and incident reporting procedure.

  6. Station 6: Teaching a junior doctor to assess a broad complex tachycardia (Circuit 1, Slot 6)

    Station Category: Teaching, Ecg Interpretation, Wide Complex Tachycardia

    Domains Assessed: Teaching, Clinical reasoning and decision making, Leadership and management, Communication including conflict

    Partner or Colleague Role Script

    You are Dr Sam, an FY2 doctor who is keen but nervous. Opening: 'I know it is broad, but how do I decide if this is VT or something else?' Answer the candidate's questions in short sentences. You can identify rate, rhythm and QRS width after prompting, but you initially jump to adenosine because you think all tachycardias are treated the same way. Become more confident when the candidate uses questions, a repeated structure and a short practice attempt. At the end, explain back what you would do if the patient became unstable.

    Examiner Checklist

    • Teaching: Checks Sam's prior knowledge and sets two or three specific learning points for the eight-minute teaching episode.
    • Teaching: Teaches a repeatable sequence covering patient stability, rate, rhythm, QRS width, axis, morphology, prior ECG and reversible causes.
    • Teaching: Explains that a broad complex tachycardia should be treated as ventricular tachycardia until proven otherwise in this clinical setting.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Distinguishes assessment of an unstable patient requiring urgent synchronised cardioversion from assessment of a stable patient who needs senior review and investigation.
    • Teaching: Explains why adenosine is not a safe diagnostic shortcut in an unstable patient and why it can be harmful in some broad complex rhythms.
    • Leadership and management: Names the escalation route to the emergency medicine consultant and critical care team and states that treatment must match the local tachycardia algorithm.
    • Teaching: Uses a short case-based question and asks Sam to apply the sequence to this ECG rather than only listening.
    • Teaching: Uses teach-back to confirm the key points and gives one specific, achievable action for Sam's next supervised assessment.
    • Communication including conflict: Gives feedback in a supportive, specific way and closes by asking Sam to summarise the stability-based decision rule.

    Model Answer

    I would begin by asking Sam what he already knows and what worries him about this ECG. I would agree three objectives: recognise instability, use a structure to describe the rhythm and choose a safe next action. I would start with the patient, not the tracing. If there is hypotension, ischaemic chest pain, heart failure, reduced consciousness or shock, the patient needs urgent synchronised cardioversion under the local tachycardia pathway and immediate senior and anaesthetic support. In this stable patient, I would guide Sam through rate, rhythm, QRS width, axis, morphology and comparison with an old ECG. I would reinforce that a regular broad complex tachycardia is ventricular tachycardia until proven otherwise and that personal pattern recognition is not a safe exclusion rule. We would look for capture beats, fusion beats, extreme axis and AV dissociation while recognising their absence does not exclude VT. I would explain that adenosine is not a routine diagnostic test and should not delay definitive treatment in an unstable patient. I would ask Sam to apply the structure aloud, correct one unsafe step and then invite him to teach it back in two sentences. I would give feedback on what he did well, name one action to practise and tell him to call me before giving treatment to a future patient with broad QRS tachycardia. The teaching closes with a clear escalation rule, not a list of diagnoses.

    References Provided

    Local adult tachycardia algorithm and Resuscitation Council UK adult advanced life support guidance.

  7. Station 7: Peri-arrest anaphylaxis after intravenous antibiotic (Circuit 1, Slot 7)

    Station Category: Anaphylaxis, Airway, Team Leadership

    Domains Assessed: Leadership and management, Practical and pinnacle skills, Clinical reasoning and decision making, Organisation and prioritisation

    Partner or Colleague Role Script

    You are the emergency nurse and team. Open with: 'She became swollen immediately after the antibiotic and is getting drowsy.' Follow clear closed-loop orders. If the candidate does not name a team leader, ask who is leading. Disclose stridor and difficulty swallowing when airway assessment is requested. The anaesthetic registrar is two minutes away. Critical care and the resuscitation consultant are available by phone.

    Examiner Checklist

    • Leadership and management: States that they are leading, confirms the diagnosis of anaphylaxis and assigns roles for airway, circulation, drugs, timing and record keeping.
    • Practical and pinnacle skills: Assesses airway swelling and voice change, gives high-flow oxygen, applies monitoring and calls anaesthetics and critical care immediately.
    • Practical and pinnacle skills: Orders intramuscular adrenaline into the anterolateral thigh using the appropriate adult dose and states the interval before reassessment and repeat.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Stops the likely trigger and states that intramuscular adrenaline is first line, with intravenous adrenaline reserved for a monitored, experienced team setting.
    • Organisation and prioritisation: Positions the patient supine with legs elevated if breathing allows, starts rapid crystalloid resuscitation and reassesses perfusion after each bolus.
    • Practical and pinnacle skills: Calls for a difficult airway plan and prepares for early intubation while avoiding a delay caused by waiting for the swelling to resolve.
    • Organisation and prioritisation: Allocates someone to document times, drugs, observations and investigations, including serial blood gases and mast cell tryptase samples according to local policy.
    • Leadership and management: Uses closed-loop communication, asks for a time-out when priorities conflict and escalates to the consultant and intensive care team.
    • Organisation and prioritisation: Closes with a post-stabilisation plan for monitored admission, allergen documentation, allergy referral and a clear handover.

    Model Answer

    I would take leadership aloud, confirm anaphylaxis and state that the trigger antibiotic must stop. I would allocate the nurse to airway and observations, another to circulation and fluids, one to draw up drugs and one to record times. Oxygen goes on at high flow while anaesthetics and critical care are called now. This patient has airway swelling, hypotension and hypoxia, so this is life threatening. I would give 500 micrograms of adrenaline, which is 0.5 mL of 1 mg/mL, intramuscularly into the anterolateral middle third of the thigh, reassess after 5 minutes and repeat if airway, breathing or circulation problems persist. Intravenous adrenaline is reserved for an experienced monitored team and must not be improvised outside the local protocol. I would position her supine with legs raised if she tolerates it, establish large-bore access and give rapid crystalloid boluses, checking perfusion and lung findings after each. The hoarse voice and stridor require an urgent difficult-airway plan. Anaesthesia should be present early and should prepare for intubation before complete obstruction. I would continue the local anaphylaxis algorithm, monitor continuously, and document every drug and time. Serial tryptase sampling may be arranged according to policy after the immediate resuscitation. Once stabilized, she needs intensive care or a high-dependency bed, allergy documentation and follow-up. I would hand over the suspected trigger, treatment sequence, response and remaining airway risk, then debrief the team and support staff after the patient is transferred.

    References Provided

    Resuscitation Council UK anaphylaxis guidance and trust difficult airway and anaphylaxis policies.

  8. Station 8: Unstable pelvic trauma after a motorcycle collision (Circuit 1, Slot 8)

    Station Category: Major Trauma, Pelvic Haemorrhage, Major Haemorrhage Protocol

    Domains Assessed: Leadership and management, Practical and pinnacle skills, Clinical reasoning and decision making, Communication including conflict

    Partner or Colleague Role Script

    You are the trauma team. Open with: 'Pelvic binder is on from the road, he is getting more drowsy and we cannot find a radial pulse.' Follow clear orders and report when a task is complete. The blood bank can activate the major haemorrhage protocol immediately. The regional major trauma centre has an interventional radiology suite and accepts the patient. Point out if the candidate gives repeated large crystalloid boluses without activating blood products.

    Examiner Checklist

    • Leadership and management: Declares leadership, assigns clear roles and uses a primary survey while bringing the trauma consultant and anaesthetist to the bedside.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Checks airway with cervical spine protection, confirms oxygenation and ventilation, then recognises decompensated shock in the circulation assessment.
    • Practical and pinnacle skills: Confirms the pelvic binder is at the level of the greater trochanters and avoids repeated pelvic springing or log rolls.
    • Practical and pinnacle skills: Activates the major haemorrhage protocol, requests blood products and tranexamic acid within the local major trauma pathway and limits avoidable crystalloid.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Requests large-bore access, blood gas, crossmatch, clotting and pelvic or abdominal imaging in a sequence that does not delay haemorrhage control.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Prioritises external haemorrhage control and early definitive haemostasis, with interventional radiology, surgery or transfer depending on the pathway.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Prevents hypothermia with active warming and warmed fluids, and states that damage-control resuscitation includes correcting calcium and clotting abnormalities.
    • Leadership and management: Makes an explicit early decision to transfer to the major trauma centre and communicates the urgency and interventions required.
    • Communication including conflict: Gives a structured handover to the receiving team and briefs the team on priorities, contingencies and post-event debrief.

    Model Answer

    I would take command, ask for silence when needed and appoint airway, circulation, procedures, drugs and scribe roles. This is a time-critical major trauma. The airway needs assessment with cervical spine protection while high-flow oxygen continues. Breathing is assessed quickly, then circulation shows decompensated shock. I would confirm the pelvic binder sits over the greater trochanters and avoid repeated pelvic examination. The major haemorrhage protocol must be activated now, with balanced blood products and tranexamic acid according to the local major trauma pathway. I would give only limited crystalloid while blood arrives and establish large-bore access. The patient also needs active warming, calcium and clotting replacement guided by the protocol and blood gas results. I would plan early haemorrhage control rather than relying on imaging alone. A displaced pelvic ring injury with this physiology raises concern for major retroperitoneal bleeding, and he may need interventional radiology or surgery at the major trauma centre. The centre accepts him and has interventional radiology, so I would arrange urgent transfer after essential stabilisation on scene and prereferral. I would not allow a CT scan at this hospital to delay haemostasis. The anaesthetic and trauma teams should prepare for transfer and possible deterioration. I would hand over mechanism, time, observations, treatment, response, imaging and outstanding needs, then debrief the team and notify the consultant in charge.

    References Provided

    Trust major haemorrhage and major trauma transfer pathways plus regional trauma network guidance.

  9. Station 9: Prolonged seizure with falling oxygen saturation (Circuit 1, Slot 9)

    Station Category: Status Epilepticus, Airway, Antiseizure Treatment

    Domains Assessed: Leadership and management, Practical and pinnacle skills, Clinical reasoning and decision making, Organisation and prioritisation

    Partner or Colleague Role Script

    You are the resuscitation team. Open with: 'He has been fitting since the ambulance arrived and we cannot get a cannula.' Follow airway, circulation and drug orders clearly. Report the low glucose and shallow breathing when measured. The anaesthetic registrar and intensive care consultant are available immediately. If the candidate gives no airway plan, state that suction and a bag-mask are ready but no one has been assigned to use them.

    Examiner Checklist

    • Leadership and management: Takes team leadership, assigns airway, access, medication, timing and history roles and asks for a time reference for seizure onset.
    • Practical and pinnacle skills: Opens the airway, suctions if needed, supports ventilation with a bag-mask and avoids forcing objects into the mouth.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Treats the capillary glucose of 3.1 mmol/L immediately according to the local pathway while continuing antiseizure treatment.
    • Practical and pinnacle skills: Orders a full benzodiazepine dose by the appropriate route and states the timing for reassessment and a repeat dose.
    • Organisation and prioritisation: Obtains intravenous access or uses the local intraosseous route and prepares second-line antiseizure treatment without delay.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Calls anaesthetics and intensive care early and states that persistent seizure activity requires a controlled airway and second-line treatment.
    • Organisation and prioritisation: Tests and treats reversible causes including glucose, sodium, calcium, hypoxia, infection, pregnancy and poisoning while protecting the patient.
    • Leadership and management: Uses closed-loop communication and keeps a clear record of seizure duration, drugs, doses, route, response and adverse effects.
    • Organisation and prioritisation: Plans post-ictal CT and neurology or critical care review according to the cause and response, with a named disposition and handover.

    Model Answer

    I would take leadership and confirm the seizure started twenty minutes ago. The airway team opens the airway, suctions secretions and supports ventilation with a bag-mask while oxygen and capnography are applied. Someone records each drug and time. The capillary glucose is low, so I would treat it immediately using the local hypoglycaemia pathway while continuing seizure treatment. I would obtain intravenous access quickly and move to the local intraosseous route if it fails. Current status epilepticus guidance directs a full benzodiazepine dose by the appropriate route, followed by reassessment and a further dose if the seizure continues within the specified interval. I would not use an inadequate dose because access is difficult. Anaesthetics and intensive care are called now because the patient is hypoxic and may need a controlled airway and second-line antiseizure treatment. While the seizure continues, I would check temperature, blood gas, sodium, calcium, renal function, toxicology risk and pregnancy status where relevant. I would look for a reversible cause without interrupting airway support. Once the seizure is controlled, he needs close monitoring, a CT head and neurology or intensive care review according to the clinical picture. I would hand over the duration, treatment sequence, glucose correction and current airway plan. My disposition is a resuscitation or high-dependency area with a clear trigger for immediate anaesthetic review.

    References Provided

    Local status epilepticus algorithm and UK resuscitation guidance.

  10. Station 10: Posterior elbow dislocation with a weak radial pulse (Circuit 1, Slot 10)

    Station Category: Orthopaedics, Elbow Dislocation, Neurovascular Assessment

    Domains Assessed: Practical and pinnacle skills, Clinical reasoning and decision making, Clinical examination, Organisation and prioritisation

    Partner or Colleague Role Script

    You are the patient and procedure assistant. Opening: 'My fingers feel strange and the pain is awful.' Say the pain is 9 out of 10. Report worsening numbness when asked. Follow instructions clearly, remind the candidate to check the limb before and after the procedure, and state that you feel faint if the limb is left unsupported.

    Examiner Checklist

    • Clinical examination: Records radial pulse, capillary refill, colour, motor function and sensory status before any procedure.
    • Clinical reasoning and decision making: Recognises the weak pulse and altered sensation as a neurovascular emergency and requests immediate senior and orthopaedic or vascular advice.
    • Practical and pinnacle skills: Provides effective analgesia and chooses procedural sedation or another approved method after checking fasting status, comorbidities, allergies and monitoring needs.
    • Practical and pinnacle skills: Positions the patient safely, provides gentle longitudinal traction with counter-traction and avoids forceful leverage during reduction.
